# Research frameworks

Guidelines for breaking a request into sub-questions. Adapt them. Drop what does not matter for the request, add what is missing, merge sub-questions when the request is narrow.

Two habits apply to every framework.

- **Pick the industry's own metrics first.** Before researching any company, name the 3 to 5 metrics this industry is actually judged on and use them for every player. Examples are ARR, net revenue retention and CAC payback for SaaS, GMV and take rate for marketplaces, like-for-like sales and sales per square metre for retail, ARPU and churn for telecom, load factor and yield for airlines.
- **Keep figures comparable.** Same year, same definitions, same currency for all players. Note the exchange rate and date when converting. Flag exceptions.

When sources conflict, prefer them in this order. Audited annual reports and filings, then investor presentations and earnings calls, then official statistics and regulators, then industry reports, then press checked against a primary source.

## 1. Market overview and market sizing

1. How is the market defined (product scope, geography, customer type)?
2. How big is it today, in value and volume, and how has it grown over the last five years?
3. How is it segmented (by product, channel, customer, region) and which segments grow fastest?
4. What drives and what limits growth (demand drivers, regulation, technology, macro)?
5. What is the realistic addressable share as opposed to the headline total market?

Best sources are official statistics, industry associations, company reports of market leaders, and market databases.
Cross-check any market size with a bottom-up estimate (number of customers times spend) when the data allows it.

## 2. Market entry

1. Market attractiveness. Size, growth, profitability, and where the profit pools are.
2. Customers. Who buys, why, how much, through which channels, and what is unmet.
3. Competition. Who is there, their shares, positioning and likely reaction to a new entrant.
4. Entry barriers and regulation. Licences, standards, capital needs, local rules, taxes.
5. Entry options. Organic, partnership, acquisition, franchise, and what comparable entrants did.
6. Economics and risks. What it takes to break even, and the key risks.

Best sources are official statistics, regulators, competitor annual reports, press on comparable market entries, and market databases.

## 3. Company analysis

1. Business model. What it sells, to whom, how it makes money, segment split.
2. Financial performance. Revenue, growth, margins, cash flow, balance sheet, last 3 to 5 years.
3. Market position. Share, key competitors, positioning, customer base.
4. Strategy and recent moves. Stated strategy, M&A, partnerships, investments, management changes.
5. Strengths and weaknesses. Durable advantages and structural vulnerabilities.
6. Outlook. Guidance, analyst expectations where public, key risks.

Best sources are the annual report, investor presentation, earnings call transcripts on the investor relations page, filings, and reputable press.
For moat assessment rate network effects, switching costs, scale economies and intangible assets (brands, patents, licences) as strong, moderate or weak, with a reason for each.

## 4. Competitive landscape

1. Industry metrics to compare on (see the habit above).
2. Market context. Size, growth, drivers, headwinds.
3. How value flows. Value chain layers for vertical industries, ecosystem roles for platforms, scale and consolidation dynamics for fragmented markets.
4. Player map. Group competitors by business model, segment, posture or origin.
5. Player profiles. One quantitative block (same metrics for all) and one qualitative block (strategy, strengths, weaknesses) per competitor.
6. Comparison. Side-by-side on the chosen dimensions with actual values, not only ratings.
7. Strategic context. Recent M&A, partnerships, funding, regulation.
8. Synthesis. Who is gaining or losing and why, where the white space is.

Best sources are annual reports and investor presentations of each player, regulators and competition authorities, industry databases and press.

## 5. Industry or sector overview

1. Definition and segmentation of the sector.
2. Size, growth and forecast with stated assumptions.
3. Structure. Fragmented or concentrated (top 5 share), business model types, barriers to entry.
4. Value chain and where value accrues.
5. Three to five key trends and the main headwinds.
6. Leading players with a comparison table.
7. What is changing (technology, regulation, consolidation) and who benefits.

Separate the hype figure from the realistic one, date everything and flag data older than two years.

## 6. Business concept validation

For a new venture or a course project business idea.

1. Problem. Evidence that the problem exists and how big it is.
2. Target customer. Who exactly, how many, what they pay today.
3. Existing solutions. Direct and indirect competitors and their pricing.
4. Market size. Top-down and bottom-up for the target market.
5. Unit economics. Comparable benchmarks for pricing, margins, acquisition cost, logistics cost.
6. Feasibility and regulation. Legal, operational and partner requirements.
7. Comparable cases. Similar ventures that succeeded or failed and why.

Best sources are official statistics for counts and spend, competitor websites and pricing pages, press on comparable startups, and academic work on the behaviour in question.

## 7. Literature review or theory section

1. Core concepts and their established definitions.
2. Seminal works (most cited, foundational).
3. Recent work from the last five years and where the debate stands.
4. Empirical findings relevant to the user's context.
5. Gaps and contradictions in the literature.

Prefer peer-reviewed journals and well-known working paper series. Report citation counts where available. Do not cite a paper you have not at least read the abstract of.

## 8. Trend or technology question

1. What exactly is the trend and how is it measured?
2. Adoption today, with data.
3. Drivers and barriers.
4. Who is leading and who is affected.
5. Credible forecasts, with the forecaster and assumptions named.

## 9. General question

If no framework fits, split the question into what must be true for each possible answer, and research those conditions. Keep it to 3 to 5 sub-questions.
