# Storylines for group projects

Pick the storyline that fits what the deck has to do, then adapt it to the material. Drop parts that the material cannot fill, merge parts when the deck is short and add what the assignment asks for. The slide counts refer to content slides (without the title, agenda and section slides).

Habits that apply to every storyline:

- **Answer first.** The summary slide comes right after the title slide and states the core message. Professors and juries remember the first two minutes.
- **Group the arguments.** Use two to four sections, each with one to three slides. Put the strongest argument first.
- **One finding per slide.** If a slide needs two titles, it is two slides.
- **Section dividers only for longer decks.** Below 10 content slides, the tracker label is enough. From 10 slides on, an agenda slide with the current section highlighted works well as a divider.
- **End with what happens next.** Use a recommendation, next steps or open questions, never a "Thank you" slide as the last content slide. A short closing slide with "Questions?" is fine after it.
- **Plan the team's part.** Mark in the storyline where the team adds its judgement. It usually belongs in the recommendation, the prioritisation and the interpretation of interviews.

## 1. Recommend a decision

Use for case analyses, strategy recommendations, "should company X do Y" and market-entry choices.

1. **Summary.** The recommendation and the two or three reasons for it.
2. **Situation.** What is true today, as a fact everybody accepts (1 slide).
3. **Complication.** What changed or what is at stake. This is why a decision is needed now (1 slide).
4. **Options.** The realistic options with the criteria used to compare them (1 slide, usually a table).
5. **Argument 1, 2 and 3.** Why the recommended option wins, each argument with its evidence (1 to 2 slides each).
6. **Risks.** What could go wrong and how to limit it (1 slide).
7. **Recommendation and next steps.** What to do, in what order and who does it (1 slide, steps).

Team input usually goes into the weighting of the criteria, the final choice when the evidence is close and the next steps.

## 2. Present findings of an analysis

Use for data projects, survey results, empirical assignments and "analyse dataset X".

1. **Summary.** The two or three most important findings.
2. **Question and data.** What the team wanted to know, what data it used, its size and its limits (1 slide).
3. **Finding 1 to n.** One slide per finding, ordered by importance, not by the order of the analysis (3 to 6 slides).
4. **What it means.** The implication for the organisation or the question asked (1 slide).
5. **Limitations.** Sample size, bias and what the data cannot show (1 slide or a footnote, depending on the course).
6. **Next steps or open questions** (1 slide).

Team input usually goes into what the findings mean and which open question matters most.

## 3. Company analysis

Use for "analyse company X", company profiles and strategy assessments.

1. **Summary.** The team's assessment of the company in one sentence plus the reasons.
2. **Snapshot.** What the company does, its size and its markets (1 slide, KPIs).
3. **Performance.** Revenue, profit and growth over time, compared with peers (1 to 2 slides).
4. **Business model and strategy.** How it makes money and what it is betting on (1 to 2 slides).
5. **Competitive position.** Its position against its main rivals, with the industry's own metrics (1 to 2 slides).
6. **Outlook and assessment.** Strengths, risks and what to watch (1 slide).

Use the company's colours if the user chose that (see `design.md`). Team input usually goes into the assessment and the outlook.

## 4. Market entry

1. **Summary.** Whether to enter, how and when.
2. **Market attractiveness.** Size, growth and profitability (1 to 2 slides).
3. **Customers.** Who buys, why and through which channels (1 slide).
4. **Competition.** Who is there, their shares and their likely reaction (1 slide).
5. **Entry options.** Organic, partnership or acquisition, compared (1 slide, table).
6. **Economics and risks.** Break-even and the main risks (1 slide).
7. **Recommendation and roadmap** (1 slide, steps).

## 5. Pitch an idea or business

Use for start-up pitches, innovation projects and business plans.

1. **The problem**, with evidence that it is real (1 to 2 slides, data or quotes).
2. **The solution** and how it works (1 slide).
3. **The market.** Who the customers are and how many there are (1 slide).
4. **Business model.** Who pays, how much and why (1 slide).
5. **Competition** and what makes the idea different (1 slide).
6. **Traction or validation.** Tests, interviews or pilots (1 slide).
7. **Plan and ask.** Next milestones and what the team needs (1 slide).

In a pitch the summary slide can be skipped. The problem slide opens instead, and its title states the problem as a fact. Team input is everywhere in a pitch, because the idea is the team's. Claude structures the argument and checks it against the material, but it does not invent the idea's features or numbers.

## 6. Explain a topic

Use for literature reviews, topic presentations and "explain X to the class".

1. **Summary.** What the audience should know after the presentation.
2. **What it is**, defined plainly (1 slide).
3. **Why it matters**, with data on size, growth or impact (1 to 2 slides).
4. **How it works or where it is used**, with examples from the material (1 to 2 slides).
5. **Challenges and debates** (1 slide).
6. **What it means** for the audience, the course or a specific industry (1 slide).

Team input usually goes into "what it means" and the choice of examples.
